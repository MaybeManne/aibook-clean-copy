L19396: 18.6.1
L19397: Application to images
L19398: Diffusion models have been very successful in modeling image data. Here, we need to
L19399: construct models that can take a noisy image and predict the noise that was added at
L19400: each step. The obvious architectural choice for this image-to-image mapping is the U-
L19401: Net (figure 11.10). However, there may be a very large number of diffusion steps, and
L19402: training and storing multiple U-Nets is ineﬀicient. The solution is to train a single U-Net
L19403: that also takes a predetermined vector representing the time step as input (figure 18.9).
L19404: In practice, this is resized to match the number of channels at each stage of the U-Net
L19405: and used to offset and/or scale the representation at each spatial position.
L19406: A large number of time steps are needed as the conditional probabilities q(zt−1|zt)
L19407: become closer to normal when the hyperparameters βt are close to zero, matching the
L19408: form of the decoder distributions Pr(zt−1|zt, ϕt). However, this makes sampling slow.
L19409: We might have to run the U-Net model through T =1000 steps to generate good images.

L19540: 18.7
L19541: Summary
L19542: Diffusion models map the data examples through a series of latent variables by repeat-
L19543: edly blending the current representation with random noise. After suﬀicient steps, the
L19544: representation becomes indistinguishable from white noise. Since these steps are small,
L19545: the reverse denoising process at each step can be approximated with a normal distribu-
L19546: tion and predicted by a deep learning model. The loss function is based on the evidence
L19547: lower bound (ELBO) and ultimately results in a simple least-squares formulation.
L19548: For image generation, each denoising step is implemented using a U-Net, so sampling
L19549: is slow compared to other generative models. To improve generation speed, it’s possible
L19550: to change the diffusion model to a deterministic formulation, and here sampling with
L19551: fewer steps works well. Several methods have been proposed to condition generation
L19552: on class information, images, and text information. Combining these methods produces
L19553: impressive text-to-image synthesis results.

L19586: Diffusion models
L19587: of writing, the state-of-the-art results for conditional image synthesis have been achieved by
L19588: Karras et al. (2022).
L19589: Surveys of denoising diffusion models can be found in Croitoru et al.
L19590: (2022), Cao et al. (2022), Luo (2022), and Yang et al. (2022).
L19591: Applications for images:
L19592: Applications of diffusion models include text-to-image generation
L19593: (Nichol et al., 2022; Ramesh et al., 2022; Saharia et al., 2022b), image-to-image tasks such
L19594: as colorization, inpainting, uncropping and restoration (Saharia et al., 2022a), super-resolution
L19595: (Saharia et al., 2022c), image editing (Hertz et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2021), removing adversarial
L19596: perturbations (Nie et al., 2022), semantic segmentation (Baranchuk et al., 2022), and medical
L19597: imaging (Song et al., 2021b; Chung & Ye, 2022; Chung et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2022; Xie & Li,
L19598: 2022; Luo et al., 2022) where the diffusion model is sometimes used as a prior.
L19599: Different data types:
L19600: Diffusion models have also been applied to video data (Ho et al., 2022b;
L19601: Harvey et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022; Höppe et al., 2022; Voleti et al., 2022) for generation, past
L19602: and future frame prediction, and interpolation. They have been used for 3D shape generation
L19603: (Zhou et al., 2021; Luo & Hu, 2021), and recently a technique has been introduced to generate
L19604: 3D models using only a 2D text-to-image diffusion model (Poole et al., 2023). Austin et al.
L19605: (2021) and Hoogeboom et al. (2021) investigated diffusion models for discrete data. Kong et al.
L19606: (2021) and Chen et al. (2021d) applied diffusion models to audio data.
L19607: Alternatives to denoising:
L19608: The diffusion models in this chapter mix noise with the data and
L19609: build a model to gradually denoise the result. However, degrading the image using noise is not
L19610: necessary. Rissanen et al. (2022) devised a method that progressively blurred the image and
L19611: Bansal et al. (2022) showed that the same ideas work with a large family of degradations that
L19612: do not have to be stochastic. These include masking, morphing, blurring, and pixelating.
L19613: Comparison to other generative models:
L19614: Diffusion models synthesize higher quality im-
L19615: ages than other generative models and are simple to train. They can be thought of as a special
L19616: case of a hierarchical VAE (Vahdat & Kautz, 2020; Sønderby et al., 2016b) where the encoder
L19617: is fixed, and the latent space is the same size as the data. They are probabilistic, but in their
L19618: basic form, they can only compute a lower bound on the likelihood of a data point. However,
L19619: Kingma et al. (2021) show that this lower bound improves on the exact log-likelihoods for test
L19620: data from normalizing flows and autoregressive models.
L19621: The likelihood for diffusion models
L19622: can be computed by converting to an ordinary differential equation (Song et al., 2021c) or by
L19623: training a continuous normalizing flow model with a diffusion-based criterion (Lipman et al.,
L19624: 2022). The main disadvantages of diffusion models are that they are slow and that the latent
L19625: space has no semantic interpretation.
L19626: Improving quality:
L19627: Many techniques have been proposed to improve image quality. These
L19628: include the reparameterization of the network described in section 18.5 and the equal weighting
L19629: of the subsequent terms (Ho et al., 2020). Choi et al. (2022) subsequently investigated different
L19630: weightings of terms in the loss function.
L19631: Kingma et al. (2021) improved the test log-likelihood of the model by learning the denoising
L19632: weights βt. Conversely, Nichol & Dhariwal (2021) improved performance by learning separate
L19633: variances σ2 of the denoising estimate at each time step in addition to the mean. Bao et al.
L19634: (2022) show how to learn the variances after training the model.
L19635: Ho et al. (2022a) developed the cascaded method for producing very high-resolution images
L19636: (figure 18.11). To prevent artifacts in lower-resolution images from being propagated to higher
L19637: resolutions, they introduced noise conditioning augmentation; here, the lower-resolution condi-
L19638: tioning image is degraded by adding noise at each training step. This reduces the reliance on
L19639: the exact details of the lower-resolution image during training. It is also done during inference,
L19640: where the best noise level is chosen by sweeping over different values.

L19647: Improving speed:
L19648: One of the major drawbacks of diffusion models is that they take a long
L19649: time to train and sample from. Stable diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022) projects the original
L19650: data to a smaller latent space using a conventional autoencoder and then runs the diffusion
L19651: process in this smaller space. This has the advantages of reducing the dimensionality of the
L19652: training data for the diffusion process and allowing other data types (text, graphs, etc.) to be
L19653: described by diffusion models. Vahdat et al. (2021) applied a similar approach.
L19654: Song et al. (2021a) showed that an entire family of diffusion processes is compatible with the
L19655: training objective. Most of these processes are non-Markovian (i.e., the diffusion step does not
L19656: only depend on the results of the previous step). One of these models is the denoising diffusion
L19657: implicit model (DDIM), in which the updates are not stochastic (figure 18.10c). This model
L19658: is amenable to taking larger steps (figure 18.10e) without inducing large errors. It effectively
L19659: converts the model into an ordinary differential equation (ODE) in which the trajectories have
L19660: low curvature and allows eﬀicient numerical methods for solving ODEs to be applied.
L19661: Song et al. (2021c) propose converting the underlying stochastic differential equations into a
L19662: probability flow ODE which has the same marginal distributions as the original process. Vahdat
L19663: et al. (2021), Xiao et al. (2022b), and Karras et al. (2022) all exploit techniques for solving ODEs
L19664: to speed up synthesis. Karras et al. (2022) identified the best-performing time discretization for
L19665: sampling and evaluated different sampler schedules. The result of these and other improvements
L19666: has been a significant drop in steps required during synthesis.
L19667: Sampling is slow because many small diffusion steps are required to ensure that the posterior
L19668: distribution q(zt−1|zt) is close to Gaussian (figure 18.5), so the Gaussian distribution in the
L19669: decoder is appropriate. If we use a model that describes a more complex distribution at each
L19670: denoising step, then we can use fewer diffusion steps in the first place. To this end, Xiao et al.
L19671: (2022b) have investigated using conditional GAN models, and Gao et al. (2021) investigated
L19672: using conditional energy-based models.
L19673: Although these models cannot describe the original
L19674: data distribution, they suﬀice to predict the (much simpler) reverse diffusion step.
L19675: Salimans & Ho (2022) distilled adjacent steps of the denoising process into a single step to speed
L19676: up synthesis. Dockhorn et al. (2022) introduced momentum into the diffusion process. This
L19677: makes the trajectories smoother and so more amenable to coarse sampling.
L19678: Conditional generation:
L19679: Dhariwal & Nichol (2021) introduced classifier guidance, in which a
L19680: classifier learns to identify the category of object being synthesized at each step, and this is used
L19681: to bias the denoising update toward that class. This works well, but training a separate classifier
L19682: is expensive. Classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2022) concurrently trains conditional and
L19683: unconditional denoising models by dropping the class information some proportion of the time
L19684: in a process akin to dropout. This technique allows control of the relative contributions of the
L19685: conditional and unconditional components. Over-weighting the conditional component causes
L19686: the model to produce more typical and realistic samples.
L19687: The standard technique for conditioning on images is to append the (resized) image to the
L19688: different layers of the U-Net. For example, this was used in the cascaded generation process
L19689: for super-resolution (Ho et al., 2022a). Choi et al. (2021) provide a method for conditioning
L19690: on images in an unconditional diffusion model by matching the latent variables with those of
L19691: a conditioning image. The standard technique for conditioning on text is to linearly transform
L19692: the text embedding to the same size as the U-Net layer and then add it to the representation
L19693: in the same way that the time embedding is introduced (figure 18.9).
L19694: Existing diffusion models can also be fine-tuned to be conditioned on edge maps, joint positions,
L19695: segmentation, depth maps, etc., using a neural network structure called a control network (Zhang
L19696: & Agrawala, 2023).
